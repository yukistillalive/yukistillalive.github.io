## Periodical Design Reflection

Around the ritual theme, we situate our context at remote birthday rituals and target our user group at young expats who are celebrating birthdays at a distance from their loved ones.

### 1 Discover

At first, we conducted a series of semi-structured interviews to engage real users. We defined the target group by setting inclusion criteria that young researchers working in Sweden after being raised in another country, and have celebrated at least one birthday in Sweden. A total of four participants were recruited through KTH email list. Participants were then instructed to submit an annotated photo or sketch that depicts their last remote birthday experience. Using these as prompts, we interviewed them via Zoom, each lasting for approximately 30-45 minutes. We asked about their experiences in detail regarding how they set up the environment as well as how they engage with people at a distance. We then asked follow-up questions to understand the underlying challenges and emotional dynamics, for instance “Can you describe a specific moment where you felt the physical distance the most?” and “Conversely, was there a moment where you completely forgot about the distance?”. To wrap up, we also looked toward an ideal scenario by instructing them to envision through the metaphor of a “magic wand”.

Parallel to these, we did literature review on related works as well. We developed a searching strategy by defining keywords in different aspects, from ritual elements to the context and user group. Then we combined these keywords and searched in the ACM Digital Library, for example, we searched (“birthday” OR “celebration” OR “ritual”) AND (“remote” OR "long distance") to review articles regarding relevant rituals in a remote context. More broadly, we reviewed studies around mediating social connection via technology, drawing useful experience from their methodological approach and study process.

If we were doing this again, we would incorporate the Contextual Inquiry method as a complement to interviews by observing their experiences under real birthday settings. To observe in the wild would allow us to capture more nuanced narratives throughout the experience compared to simply asking them to recall against photos, though it’s a bit hard to recruit participants who are having birthdays by chance. During our planning, we also considered a ‘design probe’. However, upon reflection, we realized our initial concept of asking participants to rank elements of a ritual was more of a structured task than a true probe, which, as Wallance et al. (2013) argue, should be a collective generation of unfinished objects. Thus, we just adapted this idea into part of our interview to ask for open-ended reflections while not implementing a formal probe in this discovery phase.

### 2 Define

In the Define phase, we conducted a reflexive thematic analysis on the interview data. We excerpted key quotes, identified codes on our own and merged codes into more advanced themes through discussion. Given this personal and emotional context, this method allows us to reposition ourselves as active interpreters to reconstruct participants' subjective experience and meaning. A key reflection on this process is the challenge of moving from rich individual stories to representative personas. Due to the limited participants (4), the findings might be biased and overgeneralized. The personas were therefore not intended to represent all expats, but rather to function as empathetic archetypes of the specific group we interviewed. 

With the personas summarized, we then had iterative rounds of definition on our Point of View (POV) statements and How Might We (HMW) questions. These tools made the empirical insights more accessible to design practices by scaffolding our design thinking process while avoiding jumping into solutions.

### 3 Develop

In the Develop phase, we combined multiple methods to ideate and prototype our ideas. We did not plan the detailed methodological approach from start; instead, we chose and adjusted the combination of methods dynamically along the study process.
 
Initially, ideation started with the Worst Possible Ideas method. Our design scenario is centred on “shallow communication between young expats and their families when celebrating birthdays away from home”, which is quite grounded in existing birthday rituals. Whilst this allows us to come up with ideas more easily because of the foreseeable ritual elements, this undermines our initiative to imagine the not-yet-existing reality as well. When we stick to what we already know about rituals, we would easily be trapped in the tangible imaginaries of specific artifacts, for instance a candle or a card, and would be more comfortable following existing patterns that present them in an alternative way but with slight changes. In this sense, the Worst Possible Ideas method therefore encourages us to think outside the box, leading to a series of preliminary ideas with a valuable sense of humour and absurdity, for example, an actor who can celebrate your birthday in person by following the instructions of your remote family, or an anthropomorphised pillow that represents your family.

We then chose the Challenge Assumptions method by first listing assumptions connected to the remote birthday ritual, e.g., “everyone must be online at the same time,” “a birthday needs a cake”. However, we found it more effective to use this as a prompt for individual reflection, as each of us required personal thinking time to come up with concrete ideas. We therefore shifted to a Brain Dump session, working individually before sharing and clustering ideas as a group.

We also tried the Crazy 8 method with Sketching to quickly generate more concrete ideas. Each of us sketched up to 8 ideas individually. I myself sketched the ideas by folding paper into eight segments, which helped me to focus on one idea at a time. The isolated cells and limited space relieved my anxiety to generate eight polished ideas altogether, and protect the next one from being affected by the prior ones. Also, as a student from an art and design background, I am more comfortable with thinking visually in a way that is dynamic, less formative and pre-rehearsed. Though we did not adopt ideas from this activity, this is still helpful as a critical exercise during the whole ideation phase, for me, specifically, to exercise ideas with humor and silliness.

All these sessions allow us to generate a bunch of ideas, yet not that focused. Therefore, we discarded ideas that fell out of the scope of our design context, excluded ritual elements, or failed to connect with family. Specifically, regarding the connecting feature, we also discarded the “advanced call” ideas, for example, an immersive VR room.

Reflecting on these, Worst Possible Ideas and Challenge Assumptions opened up an interesting narrative sphere for our further ideation, while the following ones grounded the divergent process in concrete, approachable ideas. Also, the strategy of dynamically adjusting methods makes the best use of the design tools to suit our own working patterns, which I think is more valuable for future practice than the work itself. The Brain Write method would be a nice practice to ensure more democratic participation, which we did not try. Our ideation process is not that critical for existing dominant ideas to be challenged and refined, due to the seemingly democratic vote session. So this method could be more inclusive to less-vocal individuals by alternating the initiatives.

Another critical lesson is avoiding jumping into specific solutions and instead starting with core functionalities. Our lo-fi prototyping process stalled due to our unclear definition of core functionalities. If we had better clarified these functionalities during the ideation process instead of revisiting them, the Develop phase would have been more thoughful by grounding ideas on the most recurrent narratives from the previous empirical study.

### 4 Deliver

We conducted iterative rounds of evaluation throughout our prototyping phase. At first, we informally tested our lo-fi paper prototype with two participants during the course workshop. We asked them to think aloud while walking through the prototype. The exercise helped us familiarise ourselves with this method and iterate the logic flow towards the hi-fi version. While building the hi-fi prototype, we sent follow-up emails to contacts on our initial list, and when recruitment proved difficult, we decided to expand the user group from young expats to students away from home. Throughout the user testing, we invited participants to test our hi-fi prototype on a real device in person. The testing included two sessions: usability and desirability. For usability testing, they were instructed to complete two predefined tasks by first sending a birthday wish to friend John, and afterwards, as friend John, reviewing and responding to the birthday wish. While participants thought aloud, another group member documented their reactions. To capture the desirability aspect, we presented them with an adjective list and asked them to select the words that described their overall impressions and the rationale of their choices.

Upon reflection, the in-person testing helped capture the dynamics of genuine emotional reactions while using our main interactive mechanism, which involves mimicking emojis to give and respond to wishes. The humorous and slightly "useless" nature of our prototype made the desirability testing particularly insightful, as it elicited strong, formative reasoning behind participants' impressions.

### 5 Terms of Engagement

Throughout the project, we used the terms of engagement tool to guide collaboration. Initially, we found it hard to collaborate efficiently as we are a bit introverted and did not assign a group leader. Later on, we rotated leadership and responsibility, and specifically, we split up tasks in the Delivery phase to collaborate more effectively. This tool helped us collaborate better on specific tasks and execution instead of having unfocused, grandiose discussions.
