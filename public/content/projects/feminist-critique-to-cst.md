# Creative Infrastructure as Ideology: A Feminist Critique of Designing Creativity

## 1 Introduction
In 2022, a visual artwork titled ‘Théâtre D’opéra Spatial’ bagged the top prize at Colorado State Fair’s fine art competition however spurred controversy as many have objected over the use of Generative AI (GenAI) in art. In this age that everyone is talking about Artificial Intelligence (AI), practitioners in creative industries emerge as front runners of using and being affected by GenAI (Sun et al., 2024). As they benefit from the escalating efficiency, creativity, and productivity these tools provide with, controversies and conflicts inevitably follow, from ethics discussion on automation of creative work (Vartiainen & Tedre, 2023), anxiety of job displacement within creative economics (Erickson, 2024) to legal disputes around copyrightability (Kyi et al., 2025) and compensation (Seger et al., 2023).

Among other practitioners, it has been a convention that artists construct and assert selfhood on their own terms, counter marginalised situations and claim authorship over their own representation. AI-integrated Creativity Support Tools (CSTs) enter this field not as a neutral instrument, but carries values from its makers and restructuring the conditions under which creative identity is built. When an artist's style becomes training data, they are already inside a relationship before they decide whether to enter or not. Yet the growing body of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) research on CSTs largely frames this as an adoption problem, persistently asking how to design tools artists will use and even engineer adoption through user psychology, however being silent about the underlying ideologies those tools carry and how this may harm what artists really value.

This essay argues that AI-integrated CSTs carry specific ideologies and conflict with how visual artists construct and maintain their creative identity. Drawing on technofeminism and broader feminist science and technology studies (STS), Section 2 introduces the theoretical framework for examining the gendered infrastructure of creativity. Section 3 interrogates specific ideologies embedded in AI-integrated CST design and traces their implications for artists' identity and labour. Section 4 reads and interprets values reflected in artists' responses as design evidence, whereas Section 5 argues that feminist design methods offer an alternative framework for CST design that centres artists' values rather than overriding them.

## 2 The Gendered Infrastructure of Creativity
Among creativity-oriented HCI studies, a growing body of literature focuses on the design and evaluation of computational techniques that support users across various creative phases (Frich et al., 2019). As the infrastructure that supports creative practice from ideation, exploration, execution to dissemination, scholars have developed tools ranging from brainstorming aids, generative design systems to specific applications, and corresponding evaluation frameworks oriented around system usability, creative output and user adoption. 

Feminist HCI scholars critique the design of these tools as reflecting specific epistemic assumptions about what creative work is, who the creative subject is and what role technology should play (Hsueh et al., 2024). These assumptions are produced by specific social actors and serve specific interests. For example, amid the technology market, a dominant position in commercial CST design treats creative work as tool-mediated expert activity which embodies a vision of technology that equates creativity with the creativity support tool itself and nudges practitioners’ adoption.
To examine implicit ideologies and their consequences for artists' identity construction, we draw from Wajcman's (2010) social constructivist account of technology, in which technological systems are understood as materially encoding the social relations of their production, and ask what values AI-integrated CST design embeds, whose interests those values serve, at whose expense, and what alternatives become visible when read through artists' confrontation.

## 3 Questioning Ideologies Embedded in AI-Integrated CSTs
With the proliferation of GenAI, CST research has increasingly focused on integrating it into specific creative workflows. Tools like Midjourney, Adobe Firefly, and DALL-E are now embedded in professional creative practice (Sun et al., 2024), and a corresponding body of HCI research has followed, with a focus on designing creative, mixed-initiative systems and exploring how AI can be a co-agency in between (Hu et al., 2025). What the integration of AI amplifies, however, is not only the capabilities of the tool but the assumptions already embedded in and the design choices carrying specific values.

In the technology market, the aforementioned vision of tool-mediated expert activity view revolves around discourses like “[tools] supercharge your creativity” “creative copilot” “[tools] unlocking creative potential”, and is heavily promoted by tech giants, from creativity-specialised industries like Adobe and Autodesk, to generalist industries like OpenAI and Google. This dominant voice implicitly suggests that the more fluently a professional uses the tool, the more creative they become. Similar emphasis is also evident in how CSTs are evaluated. Commonly used evaluation measures are established around system usability and creative artefact quality, however only a small fraction is about user-centric benefits, for example intrinsic abilities, emotional well-being, self-reflection and other self-perception measures (Rhys Cox et al., 2025). These reflect the underlying values that efficiency, productivity, and seamless adoption are self-evidently desirable outcomes, and that tools which deliver these outcomes are by definition good tools.

There is also a trend that conceptualises AI-integrated CSTs for the democratisation of creativity, which claims to lower the barriers to creative expression so that anyone can be artists. This seemingly ethically sound vision however conceals what Haraway (1988) criticised as god trick – the presentation of a situated, interested knowledge claim as a view from nowhere, omniscient and unbiased. The democratisation narrative is produced from the position of actors who benefit from maximum adoption, yet presents itself as a universal good, masking whose interests it serves and at whose expense. What it implicitly assumes is that creative identity is freely reproducible and available for extraction and redistribution. This assumed position is what makes style appropriation legible as democratisation for which we see evidence in growing lawsuits against GenAI companies, protests from creative workers and the ongoing battle over intellectual property rights.

A directly visible benefit of “escalating efficiency” and “democratised creativity” promised by tech companies is that everyone can focus on “what matters most”. Is the assumption of AI-integrated CSTs liberating productivity justifiable? Cowan’s (1976) historical analysis empirically grounded that the development of household technology did not actually liberate women from domestic work and free up their time, however increased the workload and responsibility of middle-class housewives as well as the emotional stakes of their work. Similar evidence is also seen in the trajectory of GenAI into creative industries. Not only 95% of organisations that integrate the use of GenAI technologies in their processes see no measurable return on their investment in these (NANDA, 2025), but they often end up dealing with workslops (Niederhoffer, 2025) that AI-generated poor quality work requires time to be fixed and erodes trust among co-workers. Tools that promise to free creative workers from repetitive labour instead generate new forms of invisible labour, from back and forth prompt engineering, iterating through unpredictable outputs to manually correcting AI-generated content before use (Sun et al., 2024).

## 4 Artists' Responses as Design Evidence

How artists respond to AI-integrated CSTs has implicit design significance. Prior literature documents how artists experiment, set boundaries and negotiate the use of GenAI in workplaces, where contradicting values in between imply what the tool offers and what artists yet actually need. Reading these design evidence shifts the question of CST research away from engineering adoption toward what a tool would need to offer for adoption to be genuinely worthwhile.

Despite the pervasive narrative of GenAI as transformative creative infrastructure, its uptake in practice is yet conditional and mindful. Sun et al. (2024) document deliberate non-use in situations where compliance is sensitive or content factuality is crucial, while Jiang et al. (2026) report specific low-stakes use scenarios such as generating reference images and brainstorming. The assumption that equates creativity to efficiency in using creativity support tools is challenged by evidence of extra cognitive burden and inconvenience caused by specific tools, for example, some artists have to fit themself into complicated logic of the tool using ‘cheat-sheet’ during their animation creation by Deforum Stable Diffusion, while others reported frustration, back-and-forth iterations and extra efforts in checking generated contents (Sun et al., 2024). These mindful (non-)adoption practices echo Activity Theory's account of how users appropriate tools toward their own goals rather than the tool's designed intent (Bodker, 1989), and highlight their agency in different phrases of GenAI in creative work, from tool selection to assessing its outcomes. 

Beyond merely using it for pragmatic purposes, a growing body of practice documents artists actively reinventing what working with AI means, confronting the dominant value of accuracy, efficiency and performance. Caramiaux and Fdili Alaoui (2022) report grassroots practices in AI-art where visual artists avoid falling on standard data curation and outcome measures yet embracing surprises and errors that algorithms generate. This is also evident in design researchers treating AI as cultural and political design material, likewise Arzberger et al. (2024) confront gender bias inherited from poor datasets through reflexive data duration and Park et al. (2025) develop tools for personal reflection rather than productivity.

The other assumptions of AI-integrated CSTs democratising creativity and liberating productivity reflect the concern of undervalued artists’ labour and identity. Seemingly easy-to-reproduce artworks by automation, reduced income and employment and increased public distrust towards artists (Kawakami & Venkatagiri, 2024), have altogether led to autonomous resistance and legal responses (Kyi et al., 2025) against unacknowledged human labour. Whilst some confrontations are negative through fierce protests, not all artists position themselves in the victim-perpetrator narrative among those disputes. There are also artists seeking to rebuild identity out of their own positions, for example, self-compared ‘documentary director’ to critically examine AI’s societal influence and produce new meanings through the process of crafting (Caramiaux & Fdili Alaoui, 2022), likewise a bridge among community-engaged visual storytelling practices, instead of an expert in documentary photography (Martinez et al., 2025).

From emerging agencies in situated adoption, autonomous confrontation by reflexive crafting to reconstructed artist identities, we see the creativity at stake in AI-engaged art practice, or the underlying human labour that deserves recognition, does not necessarily reside in the outcome but in the scale of aesthetic possibilities navigated by human choice within what automation makes available. What artists protect, assert, and reconstruct in their responses have expanded the meaning of creativity support to a tentative space in which a distinctive creative identity can be exercised and contested, and altogether point toward what current AI-integrated CST design has not yet been asked to meet.

## 5 Toward Feminist CST Design In the Age of Generative AI

Comparing conflicting values between the problematic ideologies with those evident in artists’ responses, we see creative labour dissolving into generic system output, concealed by metaphorical language and biased evaluation measures. We argue that an alternative approach should begin from the situated, partial standpoint of the artists whose identity and labour these tools restructure. Specifically, to counter the god trick of universal design, participatory and co-design methods can treat artists as knowing subjects rather than users to be nudged, making explicit whose interests a tool serves and at whose expense before it is built. Second, factors hidden in artists’ crafting process such as self-perception, empowerment and reflection can be considered when assessing CSTs rather than merely output quality and conventional UX metrics. Against the extractive logic of democratisation, consent, credit, and compensation should be materially embedded in the infrastructure per se, instead of things to be compensated afterwards. It’s also important to recognise and adapt the design of CSTs to a non-linear process of AI-integration, where artists’ agency transferring and identity rebuilding requires long-term engagement and negotiation. Together these moves reframe the design question away from how to engineer adoption toward how tools might be made worthy of the creative identities.

## 6 Limitations

Though the critique and analysis in this essay provide a new perspective on the design of CSTs, the work is inevitably subject to limitations. The findings are generative rather than exhaustive and may not map uniformly across creative disciplines or cultural contexts. The empirical evidence on long-term effects of AI-integrated CSTs on creative identity need further examination, with longitudinal studies can offer more insights. The feminist technology studies tradition drawn on is predominantly Western, and non-Western frameworks for understanding creative labour and identity are underrepresented both in the literature and in this analysis. Finally, the alternative design suggestions proposed in Section 5 remain largely programmatic whereas more concrete approaches should be developed and validated through design, prototyping, and empirical evaluation by engaging real stakeholders.

## 7 Conclusion

When a visual artist's style becomes training data, she is already inside a relationship before she decides whether to enter it. Drawing from feminist STS theories, this essay has argued that the terms of that relationship are designed, by specific actors, and carry specific ideologies, whereas reading conflicting values in artists’ response challenges embedded assumptions and implies alternative trajectories to the design of CSTs in the age of GenAI. We contribute by reviewing how specific ideologies embedded in current AI-integrated CSTs, how visual artists respond to what the aforementioned embedding ideologies impose on them, discussing contradicting values and identity entanglement in between, and envisioning potential design opportunities.
